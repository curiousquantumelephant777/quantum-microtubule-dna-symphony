

# $\color{#C87D20}{\text{🧅 Onions}}\ \color{#630073}{\text{and}}\ \color{#1B5E20}{\text{Photons}}\color{#630073}{\text{: Revisiting Gurwitsch's Experiment}}$

## $\color{#630073}{\text{Introduction}}$

In 1923, While investigating cellular respiration and cell division, Alexander Gurwitsch observed that onion root tips appeared to stimulate mitotic activity in neighboring tissues through a phenomenon he termed mitogenetic radiation. He hypothesized that ultraweak ultraviolet emissions from metabolically active cells could influence cell division in adjacent tissues [[1](#ref1)],[[2](#ref2)].

His experiments suggested that mitotic activity could be stimulated by radiation within the ultraviolet region, historically associated with wavelengths of approximately 190–280 nm [[1](#ref1)],[[2](#ref2)]. This hypothesis proposed a potential signaling mechanism in which photons emitted during biological processes could influence cellular proliferation. Note that this experiment has historically been viewed as controversial.



## $\color{#630073}{\text{Hypothesis}}$

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and organoid models offer a modern experimental framework for revisiting this hypothesis. Brain organoids, for example, could provide a complex multicellular system for examining whether ultraweak photon emission (UPE) is associated with measurable changes in cellular proliferation and tissue organization.

One experimental hypothesis would be that exposure to a defined photon source within the historically proposed ultraviolet range produces a measurable change in mitotic activity relative to an appropriate control condition. If the hypothesis is supported, the exposed tissue would demonstrate a statistically significant difference in proliferation, potentially accompanied by changes in cell-cycle regulation or molecular signaling. Conversely, the absence of a reproducible difference would argue against the proposed effect under the tested conditions.

Additionally, we could explore the relationship between ionizing radiation, oxidative stress, DNA damage, and ultraweak photon emission. Radiation-induced oxidative reactions may generate excited molecular species capable of emitting photons during relaxation. Characterizing these emissions could help clarify whether they are simply byproducts of biochemical reactions or whether they correlate with downstream biological effects.

## $\color{#630073}{\text{Future Directions}}$

UPE emission has since been documented in biological systems (including human tissues) [[3](#ref3)]. However, whether these emissions function as biologically meaningful signaling mechanism (e.g., in regulating cell proliferation) remains unresolved. Further research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms responsible for photon generation, their spectral characteristics, and their potential biological states. I'm especially interested in understanding the role UPE may or may not play in dynamic spindle formation and orientation states.

Mechanisms of UPE generation remain unclear. Organoids using iPSC stem cell cultures (e.g., brain organoids) could be used to test the impact of these photons and this 190-280 nm UV light source on cell division. If the hypothesis is correct, the organoid tissue by the emitter should show a higher level of mitotic activity relative to the other side. If incorrect, the two should show no statistically significant difference (or reduced mitotic activity on the side of the emitter). Also of interest is exploring how ionizing radiation may contribute to DNA damage via UPE activity.

ASML semiconductor lithography and optogenetics offer an interesting conceptual parallel in how light can influence spatial organization. In semiconductor lithography, precisely controlled light is used to transfer patterns onto silicon wafers, translating optical information into physical structure. Similarly, optogenetics uses light-sensitive proteins to regulate cellular activity and intracellular signaling with spatial and temporal precision. This raises the speculative possibility that biologically emitted photons could act as spatially or temporally structured signals influencing cellular organization, potentially including microtubule dynamics, mitotic spindle assembly, or spindle orientation. Although no evidence currently establishes endogenous photon emission as a mechanism for organizing mitotic structures, the analogy offers an intriguing framework for exploring photonic signaling and cellular self-organization.

Ultimately, revisiting Gurwitsch's hypothesis through modern molecular biology, quantitative imaging, and organoid-based systems may help distinguish between biologically meaningful photon-mediated signaling and incidental photon emission associated with cellular metabolism.
